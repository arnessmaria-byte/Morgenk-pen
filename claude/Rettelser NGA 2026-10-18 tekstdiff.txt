> I have used the image of a lion when writing about myself, and the image of a king. Beside the ruined church in 2024, I had a film figure in mind as well, and I wore the coat to match. Nobody gave me those descriptions. I chose them, at a time when my life had lost its ordinary order and I wanted something that would hold it in place.^1^
> That is what a myth does for a man in that condition. It assigns roles. The man in the coat becomes the one who stands up again. The absence becomes a trial he is passing through. The people around him become a cast, with parts they did not audition for. Disorder is easier to carry once it has been given parts. I did that, and I did it before I had examined whether any of the roles were true.
> The rowing chant I call RO is the artefact. In the author's note I describe it as my creative work and declare my interest in how it is received. The cry at the ruins I have described, in the prologue, as a prayer. Both descriptions are mine, and they do not sit easily together. A prayer is addressed to God and asks nothing of an audience. A piece of work asks to be heard. The drum, the coat and the cry appear in both, and I have labelled the same material each way depending on whom I was speaking to. I did not see the contradiction until I set the two labels beside each other for this book.
> I set this down before I describe anybody else's celebration, because the machinery is mine. A man who has cast himself as a lion is not a neutral witness to a crowd. What follows is a public scene that I enjoyed and wanted to join. I watched it as someone who had already done in private a version of what I am about to describe other people doing in public, and who had already learned how quickly a role can be mistaken for the truth about a person.

> There are no oars.
> The movement supplies them. Arms reach forward and pull back. The people taking part follow the beat and call out "Ro", the Norwegian command to row. FIFA's account of the celebration credits Ole Frøystad with the chant.^3^ The action is easy to recognise. A person can understand what to do before learning the names of the people beside him.
> I was on the other side of a screen.^4^ That distance belongs in the account. I could see a public celebration without being entitled to tell the reader what it felt like inside any one of those bodies. The men on the grass had just won a football match. Happiness at that result is a sufficient reason for their happiness. They did not owe my book a second explanation.
> Still, I wanted to stay with the image.
> The players had spent the match performing work the spectators could not perform for them. Now the distinction between skilled player and watching supporter had narrowed. Neither needed the other's footballing ability to make the same gesture. A spectator could follow the movement simply by watching. A shared action had become available without a long introduction.
> This was part of its appeal to me. So much of the inheritance I have been trying to recover requires someone to stop and explain. A name must be placed beside another name. An object has to be held correctly. A word needs a speaker who knows what it meant at home. Here, the shape of the action could pass from one person to another in seconds.
> It would be easy to mistake that speed for depth. A gesture can be learned quickly because it asks little at first. Its accessibility is useful. It also tells us very little about what the participants will ask of one another when the occasion is over. The person beside you may be a friend, a stranger, or someone you would not otherwise choose to spend time with. For the length of the movement, the differences need not be settled.
> I do not want to write that off as empty.
> There is pleasure in being able to join something without submitting a full account of yourself. Nobody taking up the motion needs first to agree with my argument about Norway. Nor must they have grown up in my family. I can value a shared act whose participants would reject much of what I believe. That is one reason the scene deserves more room than a quick reference followed by a warning about crowds.
> My grandfathers enter my response to it, but they do not explain everybody else's. Rowing already had a place in my memory because Hilmar had taken me with him. The public gesture could therefore call up something particular for me. It could remind another person of a match, a friend, a song, or nothing beyond the pleasure of joining in. My association remains mine. I cannot count the arms and multiply my childhood by their number.
> The temptation becomes stronger when the movement is given an old name. "Viking Row" can make a recent supporter practice sound as though it had been waiting in the body for centuries. The name is part of the performance. It does not establish the ancestry of the people performing it.
> No family tree is visible in that photograph. We cannot tell which grandparents spoke Norwegian, which arrived from elsewhere, or who has reason to feel uneasy when Norwegian belonging is described through blood alone. The picture contains people, not answers to those questions. It would be a strange use of a welcoming gesture to turn it into a test that some of its participants could not pass.
> The Norwegian word does give the action a particular home. Translating it does not require pretending that every place is interchangeable. It is possible to love the sound because it belongs to one's country and still make room for a visitor to learn it. Particularity becomes something that can be shared. It need not be guarded by demanding that the newcomer already possess it.
> The screen mattered too. It carried the celebration to somebody who was absent from the stadium. The same kind of device that can take me away from the person beside me could also show me a country doing something together. My account of screens has to allow both uses. I would falsify this experience if I made the image disappear merely because it complicates an argument about attention.
> Yet the view through a screen can remove the awkward parts of belonging. It is easier to love a country in a short image than to live patiently with the people who make it up. On a screen, I can leave before anyone asks anything of me. I can replay the movement without helping to organise it or staying to clear up. The fact that I am moved is real. It is also a small fact about me.
> Writing about the celebration adds another layer. I select the moment, decide where to begin, and give it a place among the stories of my family. That power is easy to overlook when the image seems to arrive already full of meaning. The chapter remains my arrangement. A spectator has become an author and is trying to persuade other people to see something.
> I have to be answerable for the persuasion.
> One way of becoming less answerable would be to call the event an awakening. Then disagreement could be treated as a failure to hear what the crowd had already revealed. Another would be to say the scene meant nothing at all, as if the absence of a national revelation cancelled an ordinary pleasure. Both readings would let me avoid the less impressive work of describing what was there and considering what I made of it.
> The picture cannot tell me whether a father went home and listened to his child, or whether somebody lonely found lasting company. Those things would require their own accounts.
> The distinction allows the scene to keep its joy. I do not need it to repair a household in order to welcome it. I can be grateful that people made something others wanted to join. Gratitude is a better starting point than conscripting them into a story they have not agreed to tell.
> The photograph holds the men in place. Beyond its edges, their lives continue. The question for me is what I do after looking at it, when there is no common beat to tell me when to move.
